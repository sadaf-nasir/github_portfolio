Lifestyle Factors and Well-Being

An Exploration of Factors Impacting Well-Being

Author
Affiliation

Sadaf Nasir

University of Wisconsin - Madison

Published

August 01, 2025

Abstract

This study examined the effect of lifestyle factors such as sleep, exercise, nutrition, social media use, social connectedness, stress management, and time spent in nature on well-being. 51 participants completed a survey through Prolific, where they self-reported their habits in each of these categories of lifestyle factors. They also rated their subject well-being and life satisfaction. A multiple linear regression was conducted exploring, establishing significant positive effects of sleep, belonging, and stress management skills on self-reported well-being F(9, 41) = 10.7, p < .001. Participants who reported better sleep, stronger feelings of social connectedness, and greater ability to manage stress also reported higher levels of well-being. These findings highlight the importance of promoting healthy sleep patterns, fostering social connections, and building effective stress management strategies to enhance psychological well-being. Implications for wellness interventions and public health initiatives are discussed.

Keywords

lifestyle factors, sleep, belonging, diet, exercise, stress management, well-being, life satisfaction

Introduction

There exists a substantial collection of research regarding the impact of lifestyle factors on well-being and life satisfaction. Researchers have explored lifestyle factors such as sleep, diet, exercise, loneliness, and perceived pressure, and their effect on self-reported well-being. One study found that sleep has a significant main effect on stress and well-being, such that the effect on stress on subjective well-being can be reduced when adequate sleep quality is acheived (Weinberg et al., 2016). Another study found that consumption of a well-balanced and varied diet, accompanied with a healthy lifestyle, plays a critical role in preservation of normal brain function and mental well-being (Muscaritoli, 2021). Furthermore, a study conducted in China found that loneliness and perceived pressure had statistically significant negative associations with mental well-being (Wang et al., 2023). In regards to social media use, one study found that social media can either enhance or diminish well-being, depending on how it is used by the consumer (Kross et al., 2021). Finally, one study found that increasing nature connectedness was positively correlated with both psychological and social well-being (Howell et al., 2011).

Considering the extensive research that has been conducted thus far on lifestyle factors and their impact on well-being, this study seeks to consolidate this research into one comprehensive study through a survey study design. It is hypothesized that factors such as sleep, exercise, feeling a sense of belonging, time spent in nature, and stress management skills will be positively correlated with well-being and that factors such as social media use and feeling constantly under pressure will be negatively correlated with participant well-being.

Methods

Data Collection

The data used for this study were collected through Prolific, an online research platform that allows researchers to pay participants to complete surveys. The survey used for this study was created in Qualtrics, and then linked to the researcher’s Prolific account. From there, the study was published and data collection began.

51 paarticipants were recruited in this study. The completed a 19-item sruvey which asked them about their Prolfic member ID (this was needed to be able to pay participants) and questions about lifestyle factors and well-being. The lifetsyle factor questions asked about factors such as sleep, nutrition, exercise, social connections, screen time, time spent in nature, and stress management. Survey questions were a good mix of categorical and numeric data types across all categories.

A data dictionary containing all variable names, survey items, data types, and units of measurement is included below.

Discussion

The present study examined how sleep quality, sense of belonging, and stress management skills predict life satisfaction. The regression model was statistically significant and explained a large proportion of the variance in life satisfaction (R² = .70), suggesting that these three factors together are robust predictors of overall life satisfaction.

Consistent with prior research, better sleep was strongly and positively associated with greater life satisfaction. This finding highlights the critical role of sleep in promoting well-being and aligns with previous literature linking sleep quality to improved mood and well-being (Weinberg et al., 2016).

Sense of belonging was also a significant predictor of well-being. Participants who reported lower levels of belonging consistently reported lower well-being. This is fairly in line with pervious research, which suggests that feelings of loneliness may have negative impacts on well-being (Wang et al., 2023). Interestingly, the magnitude of the negative relationship increased as levels of belonging decreased, suggesting a dose-response effect: the less one feels a sense of belonging, the lower their reported well-being.

Finally, the ability to manage stress emerged as a significant positive predictor of well-being at higher levels. Participants who endorsed stronger stress management skills reported significantly greater life satisfaction. This reinforces the importance of emotional regulation and coping mechanisms in enhancing well-being, particularly in the face of daily stressors and challenges.

These findings have practical implications for mental health interventions and wellness programming. Interventions aimed at improving sleep hygiene, strengthening social connectedness, and teaching effective stress management techniques may be particularly beneficial in enhancing life satisfactionvand well-being. For instance, programs that combine cognitive-behavioral strategies for sleep with group-based activities that foster belonging may have even more enhanced effects.

Despite the strengths of the study, several limitations should be noted. The cross-sectional nature of the data precludes any causal inferences; we cannot determine whether better sleep, belonging, or stress management cause improved well-being or vice versa. In addition, self-report measures may be subject to social desirability or recall bias. Future research could benefit from longitudinal designs and more objective assessments of sleep and stress regulation.

This study provides strong evidence that sleep, social belonging, and stress management are key contributors to life satisfaction. Efforts to improve individual and community well-being should prioritize these modifiable lifestyle and psychosocial factors.
